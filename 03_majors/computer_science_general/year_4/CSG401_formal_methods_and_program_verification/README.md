# CSG401 · Formal Methods and Program Verification

| Year · Semester | Credits | Level | Prerequisites | Leads to |

Part of the [Computer Science (Theory, Systems and Research) major](../../README.md). Testing shows the presence of bugs; formal methods can show their absence, relative to a specification. This course teaches the whole ladder: writing specifications, SAT and SMT solving, model checking, Hoare logic and deductive verifiers (Dafny, Verus, Kani), separation logic, and interactive theorem proving in Lean 4, Rocq and Isabelle. It ends with AI-assisted proving: what models can now do in Lean, how to check what they produce, and why the specification is still yours to write.

## Why this course matters now

Machine-checked proof has moved from research to production. The seL4 microkernel has a machine-checked proof that its C code implements its specification ([Klein et al., SOSP 2009](https://www.sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2009/papers/klein-sosp09.pdf)); CompCert is a C compiler with a proof that compilation preserves meaning ([Leroy, CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)); formally verified cryptography from HACL* runs in Mozilla Firefox's NSS and the Linux kernel ([HACL*](https://hacl-star.github.io/)), and BoringSSL includes code generated by Fiat Cryptography ([fiat-crypto](https://github.com/mit-plv/fiat-crypto)). AWS built a formal model of its Cedar authorization language in Lean and proved key safety and security properties ([AWS, 2024](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/opensource/lean-into-verified-software-development/)), runs a public challenge to verify Rust's standard library ([verify-rust-std](https://github.com/model-checking/verify-rust-std)), and describes TLA+, P and other methods as everyday practice ([CACM, Jun 2025](https://cacm.acm.org/practice/systems-correctness-practices-at-amazon-web-services/)). AI changed the economics of proof: AlphaProof found olympiad proofs checked in Lean ([Nature, Nov 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09833-y)), and on the vericoding benchmark off-the-shelf models produced verified Dafny programs about 82% of the time ([arXiv 2509.22908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22908)). But on 77 classical algorithms with identical contracts the best rates fell to 40.3% in Dafny and 7.8% in Lean ([AlgoVeri](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09464)), and a valid proof of the wrong statement is worthless. As AI writes more code, people who can write precise specifications and check machine proofs become more valuable, not less.

## Learning outcomes

By the end you can:

1. **Write** precise specifications (pre/postconditions, invariants, temporal properties) and **find** the gap between a specification and what was intended.
2. **Encode** problems in SAT and SMT, and **use** Z3, CBMC and symbolic execution to find bugs or prove bounded properties.
3. **Model-check** concurrent and distributed designs with LTL/CTL properties, and **explain** state explosion and the techniques that fight it.
4. **Prove** programs correct with Hoare logic and weakest preconditions, including loop invariants and termination arguments.
5. **Verify** real code with Dafny, Verus or Kani, and **reason** about heap-manipulating programs with separation logic.
6. **Prove** theorems about programs and mathematics in Lean 4, and **read** proofs in Rocq and Isabelle.
7. **Explain** what seL4, CompCert, HACL* and Cedar's proofs guarantee, their trusted computing base, and what they do not cover.
8. **Use** AI proof assistants productively and **audit** their output (axioms, `sorry`, changed definitions, statement fidelity).
9. **Decide** where formal methods pay off in a real project and **propose** a proportionate plan.

## Chapters and lessons

### Chapter 1 · Specifications and the verification landscape (week 1)
| Lesson | What you learn | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 What a specification is | Functional vs non-functional properties, safety vs liveness, partial vs total correctness | Write specifications for sorting, a bank transfer and a mutex; trade them with a classmate and find loopholes |
| 1.2 The ladder of assurance | Tests, property-based tests, types, static analysis, model checking, deductive verification, interactive proof; cost vs strength | Place 10 real tools on the ladder |
| 1.3 Trusted computing base | What you must still trust: the specification, the checker, the compiler, the hardware model, axioms | List the TCB of a verified program you choose |
| 1.4 Property-based testing as executable specification | QuickCheck/Hypothesis/proptest; model-based tests | Lab 1: property tests that catch a planted bug in a priority queue |

**Check your understanding:** (1) "Sorted output" is not a complete specification of sorting: why? (2) What does a proof guarantee if the specification is wrong? (3) When is property-based testing a better investment than a proof?

### Chapter 2 · Logic and automated reasoning (week 2)
| Lesson | What you learn | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 Propositional and first-order logic | Syntax, semantics, natural deduction, soundness and completeness | Prove 10 sequents in natural deduction by hand |
| 2.2 SAT solving | DPLL, CDCL, clause learning, why industrial instances are often easy | Trace CDCL on a 6-clause formula |
| 2.3 SMT | Theories (linear arithmetic, bit-vectors, arrays, uninterpreted functions), Nelson–Oppen idea, DPLL(T) | Lab 2: prove a bit-twiddling identity with Z3, then find a counterexample for a wrong one |
| 2.4 Decidability limits | Undecidability of first-order validity, Rice's theorem, why verifiers need annotations | Explain why a fully automatic verifier for all programs cannot exist |

**Check your understanding:** (1) Why is SAT NP-complete yet solved routinely for millions of variables? (2) What does "unknown" from an SMT solver mean, and how should a tool report it? (3) Which part of verification does Rice's theorem push back onto humans?

### Chapter 3 · Bounded verification and symbolic execution (week 3)
| Lesson | What you learn | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1 Bounded model checking of code | Unrolling loops, encoding C semantics; CBMC | Lab 3: find an out-of-bounds write in C code with CBMC |
| 3.2 Symbolic execution | Path conditions, path explosion, concolic testing; KLEE | Generate inputs that reach every branch of a parser |
| 3.3 Policy and configuration verification | SMT for access policies (AWS Zelkova), firewall rules | Encode two IAM-style policies and ask whether one is more permissive |
| 3.4 Bounded proofs for Rust | Kani harnesses, `kani::any()`, proof contracts | Prove absence of panics in a small Rust function for all 32-bit inputs |

**Check your understanding:** (1) What does a bounded proof say about inputs beyond the bound? (2) Why does symbolic execution struggle with loops over input-dependent lengths? (3) Why is policy comparison a natural SMT problem?

### Chapter 4 · Model checking (weeks 4–5)
| Lesson | What you learn | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1 Transition systems and temporal logic | Kripke structures, LTL and CTL, safety and liveness, fairness | Write LTL for "every request is eventually granted" and "never two in the critical section" |
| 4.2 Explicit-state checking | SPIN and TLC; reachability, Büchi automata for LTL (idea) | Lab 4: model Peterson's algorithm and a broken variant; find the bug |
| 4.3 Fighting state explosion | Symmetry, partial-order reduction, abstraction, bounding | Measure state counts with and without symmetry reduction |
| 4.4 Symbolic model checking | BDDs; SAT-based BMC; IC3/PDR (idea); hardware verification heritage | Run a symbolic checker (nuXmv or Apalache) on a small protocol |
| 4.5 From design to code | Trace validation, model-based test generation, refinement (link to CSG303) | Validate implementation logs against a TLA+ specification |

**Check your understanding:** (1) Why can't a finite test suite establish a liveness property? (2) What does partial-order reduction exploit? (3) How do you connect a checked model to the code that actually runs?

### Chapter 5 · Hoare logic and deductive verification (week 6)
| Lesson | What you learn | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 5.1 Hoare triples | Rules for assignment, sequence, conditionals, loops; consequence rule | Prove three small programs on paper |
| 5.2 Weakest preconditions | Dijkstra's wp; verification-condition generation | Compute wp for a 10-line program by hand |
| 5.3 Loop invariants and variants | Finding invariants; termination with decreasing measures | Find invariants for binary search, Euclid and an array partition |
| 5.4 Dafny | Methods, functions, ghost code, lemmas, `decreases`, triggers and why verification sometimes times out | Lab 5: verify binary search and insertion sort (sorted and a permutation) |

**Check your understanding:** (1) Why must a loop invariant be strong enough to imply the postcondition and weak enough to be preserved? (2) What does "the verifier timed out" tell you, and what are your options? (3) Why is "permutation of the input" the half of sorting most often forgotten?

|---|---|---|

## Working with AI in this course

- **Use AI for:** suggesting tactics and lemma names in Lean (Lean Copilot, `exact?`, general assistants such as Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini); drafting Dafny or Verus annotations that the verifier then checks; explaining an error message or goal state; generating candidate loop invariants to test; translating a proof idea between provers; running agentic provers on routine lemmas in P3.
- **Do without AI (drills):** natural deduction, wp calculations, loop invariants for the classic algorithms, LTL formulas, separation-logic proofs on paper, the first formalization of every P3 statement, the mid-term, final and proof oral. The statement is the part machines cannot check against your intent.
- **Verify AI output by:** reading every theorem statement against the English requirement before reading the proof; `#print axioms` (Lean) or `Print Assumptions` (Rocq); banning `sorry`, `admit` and `assume` in CI; checking that no definition or hypothesis was changed; rerunning proofs on a clean build; mutation testing specifications (break the code and confirm the proof fails).
- **Where AI still fails here (show it):** published results show steep drops from simple functions to real algorithms (AlgoVeri: 7.8% verified in Lean) and an 8.6% semantic-correctness rate for TLA+ specifications written from prose. In Lab 10 and Chapter 11 you will measure your own tools on 30 lemmas and 5 autoformalizations and classify failures: wrong statement, extra hypothesis, weakened conclusion, `sorry` left in, timeouts, and proofs that rely on a changed definition. Keep the log; it is part of P3's report.

**Mastery gate.** You pass when you can, with AI off: (1) write a complete specification and loop invariant for an unseen array algorithm and get it through Dafny within 90 minutes; (2) write LTL properties for an unseen protocol description and model-check them, explaining any counterexample; (3) prove an unseen lemma about lists or natural numbers in Lean 4 from a blank file in 45 minutes; and (4) given an AI-produced proof file with two planted problems (a mis-stated theorem and a hidden axiom or `sorry`), find both within 20 minutes.

## Industry reality

Formal methods engineers work at cloud providers (AWS publicly describes TLA+, P, Lean, Dafny and SMT-based policy analysis), chip companies (hardware verification is a long-standing market), security and cryptography teams (HACL*, Fiat Cryptography), blockchain and smart-contract auditors, aerospace and rail, and AI labs building verifiers and evaluation tools for AI-generated code and proofs. In India, demand appears mainly at global capability centres and hardware design teams (analysis; no reliable public count exists).

**Interview questions from this course**

1. *When would you use model checking and when theorem proving?* Strong answer: model checking for finite-state designs and protocols, automatic counterexamples, bounded configurations; theorem proving for unbounded or parameterised systems and full functional correctness; costs; combining them (model-check first, prove later).
2. *Write the loop invariant for this partition function and argue termination.* Strong answer: precise invariant over the three regions of the array; shows initialisation, maintenance, and that it implies the postcondition; a decreasing variant.
3. *How do you know your TLA+ model matches the code?* Strong answer: refinement mappings, trace validation from implementation logs, model-based test generation, keeping spec and code in the same review process, and admitting the residual gap.
4. *What does seL4's proof guarantee, and what doesn't it?* Strong answer: functional correctness of C against the abstract spec plus integrity and confidentiality properties, under assumptions about hardware, compiler or binary verification, boot code and the specification itself; side channels outside the model.
5. *An AI tool proved our lemma in Lean. Can we ship?* Strong answer: review the statement against the requirement; check axioms and `sorry`; confirm no definitions changed; reproducible CI build; ask whether the lemma is the property that matters; document the TCB.
